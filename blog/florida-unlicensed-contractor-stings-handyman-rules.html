Arrested at the estimate: what Florida's contractor stings mean for handyman work

Published November 2026  ·  Fanny Pack Home Services  ·  5 min read

In October, the Hillsborough County Sheriff's Office announced 50 arrests from a four-month undercover operation it called Operation Bad Builder. Detectives set up at a location in Riverview and posed as homeowners who needed repairs. They answered online ads, checked that the people behind them had no contractor license, and invited them over to quote the job.

Nobody had to finish a job to get arrested. In Florida, offering to do licensed work without a license is already against the law.

We're a handyman company in the Orlando area, so we pay close attention to where that line sits. If you're hiring anyone to work on your house, you should know where it sits too.

Quoting the job is enough

Florida law defines a contractor as a person who, for pay, "undertakes to, submits a bid to, or does" construction or repair work, either directly or "by others" (F.S. 489.105). Advertising yourself as available for that work without a license is a separate violation (F.S. 489.127).

Flagler County ran the same kind of sting in Palm Coast in 2025. Detectives worked from complaints, social media, and online ads and identified 45 unlicensed businesses. After hundreds of calls and texts, 19 of them agreed to do the work. Four gave estimates for regulated work they weren't licensed to do, and deputies arrested them when they showed up at the house. The charges included offering to work as an unregistered electrical contractor.

Both counties followed the same pattern: an ad, an invitation to quote, an arrest.

The $2,500 rule

Florida has no handyman license. It has one exemption in the contractor law, F.S. 489.103(9), for work "of a casual, minor, or inconsequential nature" where the total price is under $2,500.

Three conditions come with it:

  • Everything on the bill counts toward the $2,500: labor, materials, and all other items.
  • The work can't be part of a bigger project, and splitting one project into smaller invoices to get under the cap doesn't qualify.
  • The exemption doesn't cover anyone who advertises as a contractor or presents themselves as qualified to do contracting.

A city or county business tax receipt doesn't change any of this. The statute says outright that a business tax receipt is not a contractor license.

For a job-by-job breakdown of what needs a license, see our guide to what a handyman can do without a license in Florida.

Work that needs a license at any price

Some work sits outside the exemption no matter how small the bill.

Electrical is the clearest example. The Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) says that paying someone for even "connecting two wires" requires a licensee. Swapping an outlet, a switch, or a hardwired light fixture means connecting wires.

Our rule covers the rest. If a job touches your wiring, your water supply or drain lines, your AC, your roof, or the structure of the house, or if it needs a permit, a licensed pro does it. We don't, whether the job is $150 or $1,500.

How we handle licensed work

When we look over your list, on the phone, from photos, or in person, we flag anything that needs a license. Then:

  1. We connect you with a Florida-licensed electrician, plumber, or general contractor we've lined up for that kind of work.

Size works the same way. When a job grows past $2,500, or turns out to be part of a remodel, we tell you, and a licensed general contractor takes it.

Why it's your problem too

The criminal penalties fall on the person doing the work. A first offense is a first-degree misdemeanor, with up to a year in jail and a $1,000 fine. A repeat offense after a prior conviction, or any offense during a declared state of emergency, is a third-degree felony, with up to five years in prison and a $5,000 fine. DBPR can add an administrative fine of up to $10,000.

That felony version applies during a state of emergency declared by the Governor, which is when storm repairs pile up. Fox 13 reported that Hillsborough's sting targeted unlicensed contractors taking advantage of residents still rebuilding after back-to-back hurricanes.

Homeowners pay in other ways. The Flagler County Sheriff's Office warns that hiring an unlicensed contractor can cost you your property insurance coverage, make the house harder to sell later because of unlicensed or unpermitted work, and leave you liable if someone gets hurt or something gets damaged.

Before you hire anyone

Most of this comes from the Hillsborough and Flagler sheriff's offices:

  • Look up the license at MyFloridaLicense.com before work starts. Check that it's active and matches the job.
  • Be careful with quick-repair ads on social media, and check the license behind any of them.
  • Get the scope and price in writing, and don't pay the full amount up front.
  • Ask for proof of insurance.
  • For licensed work, ask who is pulling the permit.
  • If someone tells you a small electrical job doesn't need a license, find someone else.

What a handyman can do without a license in Florida

Published June 2025  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  Fanny Pack Home Services  ·  6 min read

Florida takes unlicensed contracting seriously. Sheriff's offices around the state run undercover stings with the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR), and in October 2026 Hillsborough County announced 50 arrests from one of them. We covered how those stings work in Arrested at the estimate: what Florida's contractor stings mean for handyman work.

This guide covers the rules themselves: what Florida law says about handyman work, where the line is, and what to check before anyone starts work on your house.

The $2,500 rule: Florida Statute 489.103(9)

Florida has no separate handyman license. Chapter 489 of the Florida Statutes governs construction contracting and decides who needs a contractor license. Section 489.103 lists the exemptions, and subsection (9) is the one that covers handyman work.

It exempts work "of a casual, minor, or inconsequential nature" when "the aggregate contract price for labor, materials, and all other items is less than $2,500" (F.S. 489.103). That one exemption is the whole legal space for paid, unlicensed handyman work in Florida.

The math catches people. If a handyman quotes $1,600 in labor and the materials run $1,000, the job totals $2,600, which is over the line. Materials count toward the cap, and so do fittings, hardware, and supplies.

The project-splitting trap

The exemption doesn't apply when the work is part of a larger or major project, and the statute specifically blocks splitting one project into smaller contracts to stay under $2,500. A $6,000 bathroom refresh can't become three $2,000 invoices. If someone pushes to keep each invoice just under $2,500 on what's clearly one project, treat it as a warning sign.

Work that needs a licensed pro at any price

The exemption also doesn't cover anyone who advertises as a contractor or presents themselves as qualified to do contracting. Some work needs a license no matter how small the bill. DBPR says that paying someone for even "connecting two wires" requires a licensee, and its own examples of licensed work include installing a dishwasher, replacing a water heater, plumbing that connects to drinking water lines, building a roof, and installing central AC.

If a repair or install needs a building permit in your city or county, check with the local building department before work starts. Local rules can be stricter than the state's.

What this means for you as a homeowner

The criminal penalties fall on the person doing the work, but you carry risk too. The Flagler County Sheriff's Office warns that hiring an unlicensed contractor can cost you your property insurance coverage, make your home harder to sell later because of unlicensed or unpermitted work, and leave you liable for injuries or property damage.

DBPR made the same point about injuries after a Volusia County sting in 2017: anyone who hires an unlicensed contractor is civilly liable if that contractor gets hurt working on the property.

How to verify a contractor's license in Florida

DBPR's free public lookup is at MyFloridaLicense.com. Search by name, business name, or license number. Check that the license is active, not expired or suspended, and that the license type fits the job (for example, CGC for Certified General Contractor, CBC for Certified Building Contractor, EC for Electrical Contractor).

A licensed contractor will hand you their license number without fuss, and most put it on the estimate and invoice. If someone dodges the question or says a license doesn't matter "for this type of job," take that seriously.

The penalties for unlicensed contracting

Under F.S. 489.127, a first offense is a first-degree misdemeanor, with up to a year in jail and a $1,000 fine. A repeat offense after a prior conviction, or any offense during a state of emergency declared by the Governor, is a third-degree felony, with up to five years in prison and a $5,000 fine. DBPR can also impose an administrative fine of up to $10,000 (F.S. 489.13).

Quick summary

  • Paid handyman work is exempt only when it's casual or minor and under $2,500 total for labor, materials, and everything else
  • At $2,500 or more, a licensed contractor is required
  • Electrical work needs a licensee at any price, and so do the plumbing, roofing, AC, and structural jobs DBPR lists
  • Splitting one project into smaller invoices to get under $2,500 doesn't count
  • Check any contractor at MyFloridaLicense.com before work starts
  • Unlicensed work can put your insurance, your liability, and a future sale at risk
